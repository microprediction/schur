An introduction

How the Schur complement bridges hierarchical and optimisation-based allocation.

Cluster the assets into two groups and partition the covariance matrix accordingly:

$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ B^\top & D \end{pmatrix}$

$A$ and $D$ are the within-cluster covariances; $B$ holds the cross-cluster covariance. The question every allocator faces is how much to trust $B$.

Where hierarchical risk parity sits

Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP) allocates between the two clusters using the diagonal blocks $A$ and $D$, and sets the cross block $B$ aside. This is a deliberate, sensible choice: $B$ is the noisiest part of an estimated covariance, and ignoring it makes HRP robust and stable out of sample. The cost is that genuine cross-cluster information in $B$ goes unused — which is the main reason HRP and direct mean-variance optimisation can pull in different directions. Schur complementary portfolios put $B$ back gradually, on a dial, so you can keep HRP's robustness while recovering as much of that information as your estimate deserves.

What the Schur complement puts back

The block-inverse identity says exactly how $B$ should re-enter. The inverse of the partitioned covariance is

$\Sigma^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} S_A^{-1} & \star \\ \star & S_D^{-1} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S_A = A - B D^{-1} B^\top, \qquad S_D = D - B^\top A^{-1} B$

$S_A$ is the Schur complement of $D$ in $\Sigma$ — the within-cluster covariance with the cross-cluster effect already absorbed. Allocate between clusters using $S_A$ and $S_D$ instead of $A$ and $D$, and the hierarchical, top-down decomposition reproduces the global minimum-variance result exactly. The hierarchy stops being a heuristic and becomes an identity.

The interpolation

Schur complementary portfolios introduce a single dial $\gamma \in [0, 1]$:

$S_A(\gamma) = A - \gamma\,B D^{-1} B^\top, \qquad S_D(\gamma) = D - \gamma\,B^\top A^{-1} B$
  • $\gamma = 0$ — plain HRP: allocate top-down on the diagonal blocks, with $B$ set aside.
  • $\gamma = 1$ — exact global MVP, recovered hierarchically via the block-inverse identity.
  • $\gamma \in (0, 1)$ — trust $B$ in proportion to how much you trust your covariance estimate. Empirically the sweet spot is interior: a noisy $\hat\Sigma$ rewards partial trust in the off-diagonal block, not full trust.

The recursion

Cluster the assets and partition $\Sigma$. Compute $S_A(\gamma)$ and $S_D(\gamma)$. Use the inverse-volatility of the Schur complements (or any reasonable allocator) to split mass between the two clusters, then recurse inside each cluster on its own sub-covariance. That is the whole algorithm. Set $\gamma = 1$ and the recursion is mathematically equivalent to solving global MVP; set $\gamma = 0$ and you get HRP back; everything in between is a smoothly tunable trade-off between estimation noise and information content.

The same idea generalises beyond the two-block case. See the paper for the recursion at arbitrary hierarchy depth and the empirical comparisons — or head back to the implementations to try it.
